## Endeavour Crater

Endeavour is an ancient impact crater about 22 kilometers across in Meridiani Planum. It became a major science destination for NASA’s Opportunity rover.

1. Review the mineral composition of exposed rocks and geological formations.
2. Survey the local crater rim and investigate the crater’s structure and surrounding terrain.
3. Examine soil and rocks for clues to past conditions that could have supported life.

[NASA Endeavour Crater and Opportunity](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/endeavour-crater-in-context/) · [USGS Gazetteer center](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/Feature/14501)

## Gale Crater

Gale is a roughly 154-kilometer-wide impact basin that formed an estimated 3.5 to 3.8 billion years ago. It is named for Australian astronomer Walter F. Gale.

1. Follow a short observation loop. Examine cross-bedding in two buttes and compare the apparent directions in which the layers dip.
2. Examine exposed rocks for geological evidence of past water activity.
3. Observe Aeolis Mons (Mount Sharp) from the local survey area and identify features that reveal its layered history.

[NASA Gale Crater age and name](https://science.nasa.gov/3d-resources/mars-trek-inside-gale-crater/) · [JPL Gale Crater size and lower Mount Sharp](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia24085-gale-crater/) · [NASA Murray Buttes imagery](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/farewell-to-murray-buttes-image-2/)

## Columbia Hills

The Columbia Hills lie inside Gusev Crater, about 3 kilometers from NASA’s Spirit rover landing site.

1. Plan a safe ascent around loose debris and compare hillside outcrops with basalt from the surrounding plains.
2. Compare the geological characteristics of five rock classes identified by Spirit: Clovis, Wishstone, Peace, Watchtower, and Backstay.
3. Look for mineralogical and geological evidence of ancient water activity.

[Spirit traverse and regional geology](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2006JE002795) · [Rocks of the Columbia Hills and Backstay class](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70030671) · [JPL site worksheet](https://marsnext.jpl.nasa.gov/documents/LandingSiteWorksheet_ColumbiaHillsGusev_final.pdf)

## Valles Marineris

Valles Marineris is the largest canyon system in the Solar System. It stretches across equatorial Mars east of the Tharsis volcanic province.

1. Read the regional terrain map and identify a route that avoids steep cliffs and hazardous ground.
2. Look for erosion, landslides, and collapse. Consider what the exposed rock layers might reveal about past habitability.
3. Examine landscape features for clues to ancient surface water and how Mars’s climate and atmosphere changed.

[NASA Valles Marineris geography](https://science.nasa.gov/3d-resources/mars-valles-marineris/) · [NASA canyon extent](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-grand-canyon-of-mars-valles-marineris/) · [USGS landslide study](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/landslides-valles-marineris-mars)

## Jezero Crater

Jezero is an impact crater in the Syrtis Major quadrangle of Mars. Its center is near 18.41° N, 77.69° E.

1. Look for geological evidence that liquid water once existed in Jezero Crater.
2. Examine exposed rocks for possible signs of ancient microbial life, including fossil-like shapes. Consider nonbiological explanations.
3. Survey the surface for a possible ancient river channel or water-flow path.

[USGS Gazetteer center](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/Feature/14300) · [USGS Syrtis Major quadrangle](https://planetarynames.wr.usgs.gov/images/mc13_mola.pdf) · [USGS geologic map](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sim3464) · [NASA on potential biosignatures](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-says-mars-rover-discovered-potential-biosignature-last-year/)

## Local mean solar time

The dashboard calculates Mars Local Mean Solar Time (LMST) at Habitat Base from the device's UTC timestamp. The map clock, mini solar card and Astronaut sheet share an update every foreground minute and immediately on resume. It follows NASA GISS Mars24 with the mission catalog's east-positive longitude convention. [NASA Mars24 algorithm and worked examples](https://www.giss.nasa.gov/tools/mars24/help/algorithm.html) · [Allison and McEwen 2000](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0032-0633(99)00092-6)

```text
JD_UTC = 2440587.5 + Unix UTC seconds / 86400
TT_minus_UTC = (TAI_minus_UTC) + 32.184 seconds
JD_TT = JD_UTC + TT_minus_UTC / 86400
Mars Sol Date = (JD_TT − 2451549.5) / 1.0274912517 + 44796.0 − 0.0009626
LMST hours = 24 × fractional_part(Mars Sol Date + east_longitude / 360)
```

One mean solar sol is approximately 88,775.244 Earth seconds. The bundled historic leap-second table uses the Mars24 polynomial for dates before 1972 and a TAI−UTC offset of 37 seconds from 2017-01-01, confirmed through 2026. A future unlisted leap second would shift the clock by about one second until the table is updated. [US Naval Observatory leap-second data](https://maia.usno.navy.mil/products/leap-second) · [IERS Bulletin C 72](https://datacenter.iers.org/data/html/bulletinc-072.html)

Both published Mars24 worked examples agree within one Mars second using their reference dates and longitudes. The minute display and the device's UTC clock bound practical precision. Local True Solar Time (LTST) additionally applies the equation of time, which can differ from LMST by tens of minutes depending on season. [Mars24 technical notes](https://www.giss.nasa.gov/tools/mars24/help/notes.html)

## Solar cycle

The calculation takes solar longitude Ls, the equation of time and planetographic solar declination from Mars24. It converts the catalog's planetocentric latitude to planetographic latitude using the IAU equatorial and polar radii of 3396.19 and 3376.20 km. [NASA Mars24 algorithm, sections B–D](https://www.giss.nasa.gov/tools/mars24/help/algorithm.html) · [JPL planetographic transformation](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/FORTRAN/spicelib/recpgr.html)

```text
φ = atan2(sin φ_c, (polar_radius / equatorial_radius)^2 × cos φ_c)
δ = asin(0.42565 × sin Ls) + 0.25° × sin Ls
true_noon_LMST = 12 − EOT_degrees / 15
H = 15° × (LMST − true_noon_LMST), wrapped to [−180°, 180°)
sin altitude = sin φ × sin δ + cos φ × cos δ × cos H
crossing_hour_angle = acos(−tan φ × tan δ)
rise / set = true_noon_LMST ∓ crossing_hour_angle / 15°, wrapped to 24 hours
daylight = 2 × crossing_hour_angle / 15°
progress = (H + crossing_hour_angle) / (2 × crossing_hour_angle), during daylight
```

The model places the center of the Sun on an unobstructed geometric horizon at 0°. Both crossings use the orbital parameters at the calculation time, so the displayed estimates can shift slightly over the sol. The model omits the apparent solar limb, atmospheric refraction and terrain obstruction. Mars24's rise/set display uses the apparent upper limb. Polar day and polar night have no rise/set pair. The stylized arc shows the fraction of the daylight interval, with a moon icon and no Sun marker when below the horizon. The two published Mars24 examples are checked for solar longitude, equation of time and elevation to 0.002°. [NASA Mars24 technical notes](https://www.giss.nasa.gov/tools/mars24/help/notes.html)

## Near-surface climate estimates

The **Climate estimate** uses MACDA v2.0, an archived global atmospheric reanalysis from the Mars PCM UK spectral model (v5.2) with orbital data assimilation. The app derives a seasonal and diurnal reference from the complete MRO/MCS Mars Year 33. It uses `temp` in kelvin at the lowest published sigma level, 0.9995 × local model surface pressure. Nearby cells have their own model elevations. The reference represents regional near-surface air. Its 5° × 5° grid and terrain-following level cannot resolve crater walls, canyon-floor microclimates, or a fixed height above the MOLA elevation. [MACDA v2.0 data and metadata](https://doi.org/10.5285/cd037a9ea387438fabf4d674dbe53088)

1. Subset the public CEDA NetCDF archive over OPeNDAP. The original archive covers MY24–35 and occupies about 201 GB. The generator reads the 23 files intersecting solar-longitude-based MY33, selecting only the nearest-surface air temperature, nine latitude rows, and the required time and coordinate arrays. It checks the level, indexing, finite values, bin counts and full seasonal coverage.
2. At each two-Mars-hour output time, bilinearly interpolate the four gridded air temperatures adjacent to each Habitat Base, wrapping longitude across ±180°. Average MY33 samples in windows of 15° of model Ls centered on multiples of 15°, grouped by the native mean solar time at the prime meridian (MTC). The MY33 table stores tenths of a kelvin.
3. At runtime, use the shared astronomical Ls and shift the base LMST to MTC by subtracting east longitude/15. Interpolate linearly across the two nearest seasonal bins and the two nearest time slots, wrapping at the equinox and midnight. Convert kelvin to Celsius and round to a whole degree for display. The bundled values are approximately 9 KB of Dart source and the app needs no network request. A missing table shows **Unavailable · No verified model**.

The temperature represents climatology from one historical model year. The estimate cannot predict the weather of the current sol, dust events, short-term extremes, ground skin temperature or point conditions. The MCD 6.2 full database requires registration and its terms restrict commercial use without specific authorization. The NASA PDS REMS and MEDA instruments separate air and ground measurements at the rovers' own coordinates and times. These archives provide observational context for Gale and Jezero. SIDERA VI applies the same MACDA source across all five bases. [MCD access and use terms](https://www-mars.lmd.jussieu.fr/mars/access) · [NASA PDS REMS](https://pds.nasa.gov/ds-view/pds/viewProfile.jsp?dsid=MSL-M-REMS-2-EDR-V1.0) · [NASA PDS MEDA](https://pds.nasa.gov/ds-view/pds/viewBundle.jsp?identifier=urn%3Anasa%3Apds%3Amars2020_meda)

**Data attribution**

Valeanu, A., Rajendran, K., Montabone, L., and Read, P. L. (2026). *Mars Analysis Correction Data Assimilation (MACDA): Atmospheric and surface fields produced from assimilated MGS/TES, ODY/THEMIS, and MRO/MCS observations, v2.0.* NERC EDS Centre for Environmental Data Analysis, 30 January 2026. SIDERA VI spatially interpolated, averaged and rounded the source values. [Dataset citation](https://doi.org/10.5285/cd037a9ea387438fabf4d674dbe53088) · [Open Government Licence v3.0](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/open-government-licence/version/3/)

Regenerate with `python3 tool/generate_macda_climate.py --output lib/missions/macda_climate_data.dart` from the Flutter project. The checked-in table contains 1,440 values. Tests use an exact grid point from this table, the mean of its four surrounding bins at a midpoint, and both periodic boundaries.

## MOLA elevations and sampling

Source is NASA Mars Global Surveyor’s Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter (MOLA) Mission Experiment Gridded Data Record (MEGDR), dataset `MGS-M-MOLA-5-MEGDR-L3-V1.0`. The `MEGT` 128 pixels/degree topography product gives absolute meters relative to the GMM3 Martian areoid (the `mgm1025` gravity solution evaluated to degree and order 50, per the tile labels). Local relief is an elevation difference between points and is outside the scope of these values. The tiles use a simple cylindrical projection with planetocentric IAU2000 latitude and positive east longitude. The spacing is 1/128° per pixel, about 463 m north–south with east–west spacing that varies with latitude. [NASA PDS MEGDR catalog](https://pds-geosciences.wustl.edu/missions/mgs/megdr.html) · [MOLA product specification](https://pds-geosciences.wustl.edu/mgs/mgs-m-mola-5-megdr-l3-v1/mgsl_300x/document/egdrsis.htm) · [NASA PDS 128 pixels/degree tiles and labels](https://pds-geosciences.wustl.edu/mgs/mgs-m-mola-5-megdr-l3-v1/mgsl_300x/meg128/)

1. Choose the `MEGT…hb.img` tile containing the exact Habitat Base coordinate and read its detached `.lbl` for bounds, resolution and sample format.
2. Find the containing pixel using zero-based `row = floor((north edge − latitude) × 128)` and `column = floor((longitude − west edge) × 128)`. Each tile row has 11,520 samples.
3. Read two bytes at `2 × (row × 11520 + column)` using an HTTP byte-range request. Decode them as a signed 16-bit big-endian integer in meters. Check the matching one-byte `MEGC…hb.img` hit count at `row × 11520 + column`.

### Maximum within the operational boundary

For each mission, enumerate neighboring grid cells and retain those whose centers are at most the mission radius from Habitat Base using great-circle distance on the same 3,396,190 m Mars sphere as the app. Each center is `latitude = north edge − (row + 0.5) / 128`, `longitude = west edge + (column + 0.5) / 128`. Read the corresponding MEGT and MEGC samples, then take the largest signed MEGT value across the included centers. A partially overlapping cell counts only when its center lies within the radius. All selected elevations were valid. The phone's Astronaut sheet displays this maximum.

## Editorial and checklist notes

The Jezero briefing uses the USGS-named crater center, while the existing Habitat Base remains the map boundary center. Possible biosignatures and habitable conditions remain hypotheses students can consider. Gale’s Mount Sharp objective is a local observation. Backstay is the documented fifth Spirit rock class for the Columbia Hills comparison. The canyon objectives treat clues about water, climate and habitability as questions for investigation.

Progress is saved on each installation by astronaut ID, mission ID and stable objective ID. The user checks each objective explicitly. Each location button closes the Missions sheet, centers the map on the assigned waypoint and selects it as the current destination. Start Navigation and PIN remain separate actions. The earlier checklist’s stored IDs remain in local preferences. The scientific objectives have new IDs because the completion criteria changed. Crew presence uses a separate Firebase session for each mission, independently of checklist progress.
